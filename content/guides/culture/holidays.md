## Public holidays

Czechia has **13 public holidays**. Offices, schools and banks close; public transport runs a Sunday timetable. When a holiday falls on a Thursday or Tuesday, many people take the Friday or Monday off for a long weekend (*prodloužený víkend*). Holidays that fall on a weekend are **not** moved to a weekday.

| Date | Czech name | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| 1 January | *Den obnovy samostatného českého státu; Nový rok* | Restoration of the independent Czech state (1993), and New Year's Day |
| Friday before Easter | *Velký pátek* | Good Friday |
| Monday after Easter | *Velikonoční pondělí* | Easter Monday: the day of the *pomlázka* (see below) |
| 1 May | *Svátek práce* | Labour Day, and the traditional day for lovers |
| 8 May | *Den vítězství* | Victory Day, end of the Second World War in Europe |
| 5 July | *Den slovanských věrozvěstů Cyrila a Metoděje* | Saints Cyril and Methodius, who brought Christianity and writing in 863 |
| 6 July | *Den upálení mistra Jana Husa* | Anniversary of the burning of Jan Hus in 1415 |
| 28 September | *Den české státnosti* | Czech Statehood Day: the feast of St Wenceslas (*svatý Václav*) |
| 28 October | *Den vzniku samostatného československého státu* | Founding of Czechoslovakia in 1918, the main national day |
| 17 November | *Den boje za svobodu a demokracii* | Struggle for Freedom and Democracy Day: the student protests of 1939 and the Velvet Revolution of 1989 |
| 24 December | *Štědrý den* | Christmas Eve: **the** day of Czech Christmas |
| 25 December | *1. svátek vánoční* | Christmas Day |
| 26 December | *2. svátek vánoční* | St Stephen's Day |

On 1 January, Easter Monday, 8 May, 28 September, 28 October and 25–26 December larger shops (over 200 m²) are closed by law, and on 24 December they close at midday; shops at stations and airports, pharmacies and petrol stations are exempt (ČOI, checked 17 November 2026). Plan your shopping.

Each holiday has a short prep lesson (words, customs, what to say): see [Czech holidays](/holidays). The one coming up is featured on your home screen the week before.

## Other days you'll notice

These aren't days off, but you'll see and hear them:

- **Masopust** (February): carnival before Lent, with masked processions in villages and some Prague neighbourhoods, and plenty of pork and doughnuts.
- **Pálení čarodějnic** (30 April, "burning of the witches"): bonfires all over the country, often with a straw witch on top and sausages roasted over the fire.
- **První máj** (1 May): couples kiss under a blossoming tree, usually a cherry, for luck in love. In Prague, many go to the statue of the poet Karel Hynek Mácha on Petřín Hill, author of the poem *Máj*.
- **Dušičky** (2 November, All Souls' Day): families light candles on graves; cemeteries glow in the evening.
- **Mikuláš** (evening of 5 December): St Nicholas walks the streets with an angel (*anděl*) and a devil (*čert*). Children recite a poem or sing a song and get sweets, or, as a joke, coal or potatoes if they've been naughty.
- **Silvestr** (31 December): New Year's Eve, with fireworks at midnight. People send **PF cards** (from French *pour féliciter*, "to congratulate"): *PF 2027* means "Happy New Year 2027".
- **Name days**: see [Everyday life and work](/guides/culture).

## Czech Christmas

Christmas is the biggest family celebration of the year, and the main event is the evening of **24 December**.

- **Advent:** four Sundays of lighting candles on an advent wreath; Christmas markets on town squares, with mulled wine (*svařák*) and honey wine (*medovina*).
- **Cukroví:** in the weeks before Christmas, families bake ten or more kinds of small Christmas biscuits, such as *vanilkové rohlíčky* (vanilla crescents) and *linecké* (jam sandwiches). Colleagues bring boxes of them to the office.
- **The carp:** in the days before Christmas, live carp are sold from big tubs on the streets. Some families keep theirs in the bath until Christmas Eve, and some let it go in the river.
- **Christmas Eve:** some people fast all day in the hope of seeing the *zlaté prasátko* (golden piglet), a sign of luck. Dinner is usually **fish soup** and **fried carp with potato salad** (*smažený kapr s bramborovým salátem*).
- **Presents** are brought on Christmas Eve by **Ježíšek** (the baby Jesus), not Santa. After dinner a little bell rings, and the children run to find presents under the tree.
- **Fortune-telling customs:** cutting an apple crosswise (a star inside means health for the coming year), floating walnut-shell boats with candles, and putting a carp scale in your wallet so the money keeps coming.
- **25–26 December** are for visiting the rest of the family.

## Czech Easter

Easter (*Velikonoce*, always plural) is mostly a spring folk festival.

- Eggs are painted and decorated (*kraslice*), and houses are decorated with willow branches and ribbons.
- On **Easter Monday** morning, boys and men go from house to house with a **pomlázka**, a whip braided from willow twigs, and give the girls and women a light symbolic swat on the legs "so they stay healthy and young all year". They recite a rhyme (*koleda*), such as:

  > *Hody, hody, doprovody, dejte vejce malovaný.*
  > *Nedáte-li malovaný, dejte aspoň bílý,*
  > *slepička vám snese jiný.*
  >
  > (Hey, hey, give us a painted egg. If you won't give a painted one, give at least a white one: the hen will lay you another.)

- In return they get painted eggs, sweets and a ribbon tied to the pomlázka; grown men are often offered a shot of something strong. In some regions, girls get their revenge in the afternoon with a bucket of water.
- The custom is mostly practised in families and villages and is understood as a playful tradition, although some people, especially in cities, find it outdated. It is only done among people who know each other.
