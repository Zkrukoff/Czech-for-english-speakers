## Why history comes up at work

Czech public holidays, street names, Prague's monuments and a good deal of everyday humour all refer back to a few turning points. Knowing them helps you follow conversations, understand why a colleague suddenly has the day off, and see why Czechs value their language and independence so highly.

## Timeline

| When | What happened | Czech term |
|---|---|---|
| 863 | The missionaries **Cyril and Methodius** arrive in Great Moravia, bringing Christianity and the first Slavic alphabet. | *Cyril a Metoděj* |
| 10th century | Duke **Wenceslas** (Václav), later the patron saint of the Czech lands, is murdered by his brother (929 or 935). | *svatý Václav* |
| 1346–1378 | **Charles IV**, King of Bohemia and Holy Roman Emperor, makes Prague an imperial capital: Charles University (1348), the New Town, Charles Bridge (1357). | *Karel IV.* |
| 1415 | The reformer **Jan Hus**, who preached in Czech and criticised the Church, is burned at the stake in Konstanz. The Hussite wars follow. | *mistr Jan Hus, husité* |
| 1620 | Defeat of the Protestant Czech estates at the **Battle of White Mountain**. In 1621, 27 leaders are executed on Old Town Square. Three centuries of Habsburg rule and Germanisation follow. | *Bílá hora* |
| late 18th–19th century | The **National Revival**: writers and scholars bring Czech back as a language of literature and science. The National Theatre opens in 1883, paid for by public collections: *Národ sobě* ("The nation to itself"). | *národní obrození* |
| 28 October 1918 | **Czechoslovakia** is founded after the collapse of Austria-Hungary. Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk becomes the first president. | *vznik Československa* |
| 1938 | The **Munich Agreement**: Britain, France, Germany and Italy give the Czech border regions to Nazi Germany without Czechoslovakia at the table. Czechs still call it *"o nás bez nás"*: about us, without us. | *Mnichovská dohoda* |
| 1939–1945 | **Nazi occupation** (the "Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia"). On 17 November 1939 the Nazis close Czech universities after student protests. In 1942, Czechoslovak paratroopers assassinate Reinhard Heydrich; the village of Lidice is destroyed in revenge. | *okupace, protektorát* |
| 5–9 May 1945 | The **Prague Uprising** and the end of the war in Europe. | *Pražské povstání, osvobození* |
| February 1948 | The **Communist Party takes power**. Forty-one years of one-party rule begin. | *Únor 1948* |
| 1968 | The **Prague Spring**, an attempt at "socialism with a human face" under Alexander Dubček, is crushed by the Warsaw Pact invasion on 21 August. | *Pražské jaro, okupace 1968* |
| January 1969 | The student **Jan Palach** sets himself on fire on Wenceslas Square in protest against the resignation that followed the invasion. | *Jan Palach* |
| 1977 | Dissidents, including the playwright Václav Havel, sign **Charter 77**, demanding that the regime respect human rights. | *Charta 77* |
| 17 November 1989 | Police beat a peaceful student march in Prague, sparking the **Velvet Revolution**. Within weeks the regime falls; Václav Havel becomes president. | *sametová revoluce* |
| 1 January 1993 | Czechoslovakia splits peacefully into **Czechia and Slovakia**, the "Velvet Divorce". | *rozdělení Československa* |
| 1999 / 2004 | Czechia joins **NATO** (1999) and the **European Union** (2004). | *NATO, Evropská unie* |

## How it shows up today

- **Language.** The National Revival is why Czech is fiercely cherished, and why Czechs are genuinely pleased when foreigners learn it. It's also why many everyday words are home-grown rather than international (*divadlo* for theatre, *kyslík* for oxygen).
- **Public holidays.** Seven of the thirteen are anniversaries from this timeline: Cyril and Methodius (5 July), Jan Hus (6 July), St Wenceslas (28 September), the founding of Czechoslovakia (28 October), the fight for freedom and democracy on 17 November (both 1939 and 1989), Victory Day (8 May), and the restoration of the independent Czech state (1 January).
- **Attitudes.** Centuries of rule from elsewhere, Munich, 1968 and the communist decades left a deep scepticism of authority and of grand promises, and a talent for irony, which Švejk embodies. They also left strong feelings about 1968 and Russia, so tread carefully with jokes about either.
- **Havel's legacy.** His line *"Pravda a láska musí zvítězit nad lží a nenávistí"* ("Truth and love must prevail over lies and hatred") is still quoted, sometimes sincerely and sometimes ironically.

## History you can see in Prague

| Place | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Wenceslas Square** (*Václavské náměstí*) | The statue of St Wenceslas; the memorial to Jan Palach and Jan Zajíc in the pavement near the National Museum; the scene of the 1989 demonstrations. |
| **Národní třída** | A small memorial with raised hands on the arcade where the march of 17 November 1989 was stopped. People light candles here every 17 November. |
| **Old Town Square** (*Staroměstské náměstí*) | The Jan Hus monument; 27 white crosses in the pavement by the Old Town Hall for the leaders executed in 1621. |
| **Church of Sts Cyril and Methodius**, Resslova street | The crypt where the Heydrich paratroopers made their last stand in 1942, with bullet marks still in the wall. |
| **Charles Bridge** and the Old Town Bridge Tower | Charles IV's Prague. |
| **National Theatre** (*Národní divadlo*) | *Národ sobě* above the stage: the National Revival in gold letters. |
