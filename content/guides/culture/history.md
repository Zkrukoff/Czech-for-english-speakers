## Why history comes up at work

Czech public holidays, street names, Prague's monuments and a good deal of everyday humour all refer back to a few turning points. Knowing them helps you follow conversations, understand why a colleague suddenly has the day off, and see why Czechs value their language and independence so highly.

## How it shows up today

- **Language.** The National Revival is why Czech is fiercely cherished, and why Czechs are genuinely pleased when foreigners learn it. It's also why many everyday words are home-grown rather than international (*divadlo* for theatre, *kyslík* for oxygen).
- **Public holidays.** Seven of the thirteen are anniversaries from this timeline: Cyril and Methodius (5 July), Jan Hus (6 July), St Wenceslas (28 September), the founding of Czechoslovakia (28 October), the fight for freedom and democracy on 17 November (both 1939 and 1989), Victory Day (8 May), and the restoration of the independent Czech state (1 January).
- **Attitudes.** Centuries of rule from elsewhere, Munich, 1968 and the communist decades left a deep scepticism of authority and of grand promises, and a talent for irony, which Švejk embodies. They also left strong feelings about 1968 and Russia, so tread carefully with jokes about either.
- **Havel's legacy.** His line *"Pravda a láska musí zvítězit nad lží a nenávistí"* ("Truth and love must prevail over lies and hatred") is still quoted, sometimes sincerely and sometimes ironically.
