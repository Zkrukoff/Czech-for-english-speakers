## Saying hello to everyone

Czechs greet people they don't know in small, shared spaces. Say ***Dobrý den*** when you walk into a small shop, a doctor's waiting room, a lift or a train compartment, and ***Na shledanou*** when you leave. Staying silent can come across as rude, even though nobody expects a conversation afterwards.

## Formality, titles and e-mails

Czech workplaces can be more formal than English-speaking ones, especially in public institutions, traditional companies and with older colleagues.

- **Ty and vy:** start with *vy* and *Dobrý den*, and let the other person suggest *ty* (*Můžeme si tykat?*). International companies and IT often use *ty* from the first day. See unit 4, lesson 4.
- **Academic titles matter.** You'll see them on doorbells, business cards and e-mail signatures: *Ing.* (engineer), *Mgr.* (master's), *MUDr.* (doctor of medicine), *PhDr.*, *doc.*, *prof.* In formal situations people are addressed by title: *Dobrý den, pane inženýre. Paní doktorko, …*
- **E-mails** start with ***Dobrý den,*** (formal, and safe with almost anyone) or ***Vážená paní…, Vážený pane…*** (very formal), and end with ***S pozdravem*** (Kind regards) and your name. Between colleagues on *ty*: *Ahoj Petře,* … *Díky, Daniel*.

## Name days and birthdays

Almost every day of the year belongs to one or more names in the Czech calendar: Jana on 24 May, Petr on 29 June, Václav on 28 September. Your **name day** (*svátek* or *jmeniny*) is celebrated almost like a second birthday: colleagues and family congratulate you with ***Všechno nejlepší k svátku!***

At work, **the person celebrating brings the treat**: a cake, cookies, or a tray of *chlebíčky* (open sandwiches), often with a bottle of something for after work. The same goes for birthdays, especially round ones (*kulatiny*: 30, 40, 50…). If your name isn't in the Czech calendar, colleagues may ask when you'd like to celebrate instead.

## Visiting someone's home

- **Take your shoes off** at the door. Your hosts will usually offer you slippers (*bačkory*, *papuče*).
- Bring something small: flowers, a bottle of wine, chocolates.
- When glasses are raised, say ***Na zdraví!*** (to health) and **look each person in the eye** as you clink. Folk wisdom says that looking away brings seven years of bad luck.

## Food and drink

- **Lunch** is the main meal of the working day, often a soup and a main course from the *polední menu*.
- **In pubs** the waiter keeps a tally of your beers on a slip of paper or the beer mat (*pivní tácek*). A new beer often arrives without asking until you put the mat on top of your glass.
- **Sunday lunch** at home is traditionally something like *svíčková* (beef in a creamy vegetable sauce with dumplings) or roast pork with dumplings and sauerkraut (*vepřo knedlo zelo*).

## Weekends

Many Czech families have a **cottage** in the country (*chata* or *chalupa*) and leave the city on Friday afternoon. Other national pastimes:

- **Hiking** on routes marked with red, blue, green and yellow paint stripes on trees and rocks (*turistické značky*), maintained by volunteers for over a century.
- **Mushroom picking** (*houbaření*): in a good season, half the country is in the forest on Sunday morning, and everyone has a favourite secret spot.

## Humour and conversation

Czech humour is **dry, ironic and self-deprecating**. The classic reference is the novel *The Good Soldier Švejk*, whose hero survives the absurd by appearing to obey it. Some things to know:

- *Jak se máš?* is a real question, often answered honestly: *Nic moc* (not great) is a normal answer.
- Complaining together, about the weather, the government or the trams, is a way of bonding.
- Compliments are often brushed aside (*Ale prosím vás, to nic nebylo*: "oh please, it was nothing").
- Czechs can seem reserved with strangers but are warm once they know you. Trying to speak Czech, even badly, is noticed and appreciated.

## Religion

Czechia is one of the least religious countries in Europe. Christmas and Easter are celebrated mainly as **family and folk traditions**, and most Czechs won't assume you're religious either.

## Shops on public holidays

By law, **larger shops** (over 200 m²) close on 1 January, Easter Monday, 8 May, 28 September, 28 October, 25 and 26 December, and from midday on 24 December. Small shops, petrol stations and shops at stations and airports can stay open. On other public holidays most shops open as usual. See [Holidays](/guides/culture?tab=holidays).
