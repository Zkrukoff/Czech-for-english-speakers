## Describing appearance: je… / má…

Czech describes people with two verbs, as English does:

| být (is) + adjective | mít (has) + accusative |
|---|---|
| Je **vysoký**. He's tall. | Má **krátké vlasy**. He has short hair. |
| Je **vysoká**. She's tall. | Má **brýle**. She has glasses. |
| Je **mladý**. He's young. | Má **vousy**. He has a beard. |

To ask: ***Jak vypadá?*** What does he/she look like? (literally "How does he look?")

## Adjectives agree

An adjective takes the gender and number of its noun. For hard adjectives in the nominative:

| | masculine | feminine | neuter |
|---|---|---|---|
| singular | vysok**ý** muž | vysok**á** žena | vysok**é** dítě |
| plural | vysoc**í** muži (people) / vysok**é** stromy (things) | vysok**é** ženy | vysok**á** města |

## Hair, glasses, beard: plural words

Some things English treats as singular or "a pair of" are plural in Czech: **vlasy** (hair), **brýle** (glasses), **vousy** (beard). Adjectives with them are plural too: *krátké vlasy, nové brýle*.

After *má* they're in the accusative, which for these plurals looks the same as the nominative: *Má dlouhé vlasy.* But a feminine singular object changes: *Má modr**ou** košil**i**.* (modrá košile)

## Being tactful

Czechs are fairly direct, but with appearance people soften things: ***spíš menší*** (rather short) rather than *malý*, ***silnější*** (stronger, i.e. heavier) rather than *tlustý* (fat).

> **Heard on the street:** in spoken Czech, plural adjectives often end in **-ý** for everything: *má dlouhý vlasy* (standard: *dlouhé vlasy*).
